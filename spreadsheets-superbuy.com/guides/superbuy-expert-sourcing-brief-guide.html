Superbuy Expert Sourcing: How to Write a Supplier-Ready Brief

A reference photo can start a search, but it cannot define the product. Turn your idea into measurable requirements before asking an expert to find, customize or purchase it.
FIELD GUIDE

Published September 8, 2026

15 min read

← All articles
01

Use expert sourcing for the right problem

A normal shopping-agent order works best when you already have a clear product page and can select the required option. Expert sourcing begins earlier. Superbuy’s current public page presents the service for finding a product from information such as a reference image and size, handling small-quantity customization requests, and purchasing through multiple e-commerce platforms, including third-party platforms that may be awkward to order from directly.

That does not mean every uncertain purchase needs an expert. If the exact listing, variation and quantity are known, the ordinary order flow is simpler. Use sourcing when the central task is discovery or specification: locating a similar item, finding a supplier that accepts a small run, checking whether a requested logo or dimension is feasible, or organizing a purchase where the platform is not well suited to the standard form. Write the problem in one sentence before submitting anything. If the sentence is “buy this exact blue option from this link,” you probably have a normal order. If it is “find a food-safe version of this design in a specific capacity and quantity,” you have a sourcing brief.

02

Separate requirements from preferences

The fastest way to produce an unusable quote is to call every detail “important.” Divide the brief into three levels. Non-negotiables are conditions that make the product acceptable: dimensions, material, compatibility, safety characteristic, quantity or delivery constraint. Preferences improve the result but can be traded: shade, surface finish, packaging style or a target price. Reference features explain what you like in an example without requiring a copy.

Put measurable requirements first. Replace “large” with a dimension range, “premium fabric” with fiber content and weight if known, and “same as photo” with the exact features the photo is meant to demonstrate. State acceptable tolerances when they matter. A supplier can answer “diameter 82–85 mm”; it cannot reliably price “about this size.” Also list exclusions. If batteries, magnets, liquids, untreated wood or branded packaging would make international routing difficult, say that those features are not acceptable. This lets sourcing filter candidates before you spend time comparing products that cannot complete the later journey.

03

Build a reference pack that explains itself

Superbuy asks users to provide product information such as size and an image so the request can be processed. Treat the image as evidence, not the specification. Supply one clean overall reference, then add close views only when they clarify construction, texture, connector shape, closure, print placement or another decision. Mark which image shows appearance and which shows a required function. Images collected from different products can easily contradict one another unless their purpose is labelled.

Create a short reference index in your spreadsheet: file name, feature demonstrated, required or preferred, and notes. For a bag, one image might show the silhouette, another the strap attachment, and a third the interior pockets. If color is critical, name a recognized color reference or provide a physical sample plan; screen colors shift between devices. Remove seller watermarks and personal information only when you have the right to do so, but do not crop away scale clues. A ruler, dimension sketch or known object can make the reference more useful than another beauty shot.

04

Define quantity, sample stage and customization

Small-quantity customization is one of the uses described on Superbuy’s Expert Sourcing page. “Small” is not a purchasing number, however. State the sample quantity, intended production quantity and whether repeat orders are possible. Suppliers may have different minimums for an existing product, a custom color, printed packaging, a new mold or a logo application. Those are separate changes and should be priced separately.

Break customization into components: base product, material or color change, artwork method, artwork position and size, packaging, labeling, and any accessory. Specify whether you need a plain sample before branding. A sensible sequence is availability check, unbranded sample, specification approval, branded sample when necessary, then the production quantity. Do not approve a large order from a digital mockup alone when fit, finish or function matters. Ask the sourcing response to identify setup charges, sample cost, unit price, domestic delivery, minimum quantity and estimated preparation time as separate fields. That structure prevents a low headline unit price from hiding the real entry cost.

05

Set a budget that can be evaluated

A target price without quantity, specification and included costs is not a useful ceiling. Record the target unit price and clarify whether it includes customization, domestic delivery to the Superbuy warehouse, packaging and taxes charged by the seller. Keep international shipping outside the supplier quote because it depends on the packed parcel, destination and route available later.

Use three budget values: preferred unit price, maximum acceptable unit price, and maximum total for the sample or first run. Then state which preference can change if the quote is too high. Perhaps a stock color is acceptable, individual retail boxes can be removed, or the quantity can rise within a limit. Never ask for “best price” without stating what must stay fixed; the cheapest response may describe a different material or incomplete package. Compare quotes in matched columns and flag every blank. An unanswered material grade or packaging detail is not automatically included just because another supplier included it.

06

Ask for evidence before choosing a candidate

A sourcing result is a candidate, not a final quality verdict. Superbuy’s broader guidance explains that purchases from third-party platforms can carry product, quality and intellectual-property risks. Your decision sheet should therefore separate what the listing claims, what the supplier confirms, what a sample demonstrates and what warehouse photos can later verify.

For an existing product, request current listing photos, variation details and the exact product link or platform reference when available. For customization, request evidence relevant to the proposed method: a comparable finished sample, material specification, dimensional drawing or packaging example. Check whether the candidate actually answers every non-negotiable. A polished photo does not resolve missing dimensions. If two offers appear identical but one is much cheaper, compare material, included accessories, packaging, minimum quantity and domestic freight before assuming it is a bargain. Record uncertainties as questions and resolve them before payment rather than converting them into optimistic assumptions.

07

Turn the chosen result into an order record

Once a candidate is selected, freeze the approved specification. Give the version a date and copy the final choices into one order record: product, quantity, dimensions, material, color, customization, artwork version, packaging, agreed unit price, domestic delivery and any approved sample. Do not let the decisive requirements remain scattered across chat messages and images.

Use a comparison checkpoint before payment. The order option must match the approved sourcing result, and any change should be written as a revision rather than silently substituted. Save the supplier or platform description that supports each key field. If the product will be customized, confirm spelling and artwork orientation carefully; warehouse inspection cannot undo production. When goods later reach the warehouse, compare the intake evidence with this frozen record. Standard photos may show the overall item, but a specific measurement, internal detail or print position may require an appropriate inspection request. Decide those evidence needs while writing the sourcing brief, not after the goods arrive.

08

Use a one-page sourcing brief

A good request can fit on one page even when the decision behind it is detailed. Start with a plain-language product name and the intended use. Follow with non-negotiable specifications, preferred features, quantity and sample plan, customization details, target budget, reference-image index, exclusions and the questions the response must answer. Finish with a short acceptance test: what facts must be confirmed before you will approve the candidate?

Keep a sourcing log beside the brief with submission date, response date, candidate, unanswered fields, revision number and decision. This protects you from comparing an early quote against a later specification. It also makes rejection productive: note whether a candidate failed because of material, minimum quantity, cost, customization method or platform risk. The objective is not to receive the largest number of links. It is to reduce an open-ended idea to one supplier-ready, purchasable and inspectable product definition. Expert help becomes more effective when the buyer has already made the important tradeoffs visible.

PRACTICAL FAQ

Questions buyers ask before submitting a sourcing request

When should I use Expert Sourcing instead of a normal order? +

Use it when you need help finding the product, arranging small-quantity customization or purchasing through a platform that does not fit the ordinary order workflow. Use a normal order when the exact listing and option are already clear.

Is one reference image enough? +

It can start the request, but dimensions, material, quantity and required features should be written separately. An image rarely defines all of those facts.

How should I describe a target price? +

State the unit-price target, quantity and what the figure should include. Also provide a maximum total for samples or the first run.

Should I request a sample before customization? +

When function, fit, texture or construction matters, an unbranded sample can verify the base product before you pay for artwork or a larger run.

Can I ask for a custom logo? +

Superbuy publicly lists small-quantity customization as an Expert Sourcing use case. Feasibility, minimum quantity, method and cost still depend on the supplier and request.

What makes two supplier quotes comparable? +

Compare the same material, dimensions, customization, packaging, quantity, setup charges and domestic delivery. Treat missing fields as unresolved.

Does a sourcing result guarantee product quality? +

No. Treat it as a candidate. Verify the specification, platform information, sample evidence and later warehouse inspection appropriate to the risk.

What should I save after selecting a product? +

Keep the final specification version, reference images, chosen option, quantity, confirmed price components, supplier evidence and required inspection checks.
