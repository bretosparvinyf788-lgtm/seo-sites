Delivery Evidence

OOPBuy Parcel Delivery Inspection Guide: Build a 72-Hour Evidence File

Inspect the shipment before acceptance, record one defensible unboxing and turn a shortage or damage problem into a clear support case.

Published 2026-09-0212 min read1,660 wordsFact-checked against OOPBuy official pages · September 2026

The riskiest moment in an international shopping-agent order is often the least planned: the parcel is finally at the door, the buyer is excited, and the box is opened before anyone records its condition. If an item is missing or damaged, the useful evidence may already be gone. A photo taken after everything has been moved cannot show how the shipment arrived.

OOPBuy's public parcel-receiving notice tells recipients to inspect the outer packaging, sealed-box condition and any obvious weight difference before signing. Its after-delivery guidance says missing, damaged, defective and similar product issues should be reported to customer service within 72 hours after receipt. Those instructions make delivery a controlled handoff, not merely the end of tracking.

This guide turns that handoff into a repeatable inspection. It does not promise compensation. OOPBuy states that parcel loss or damage is handled under the compensation standards of the selected line, so eligibility and amounts can differ. The buyer's job is to preserve facts quickly enough for the right party to investigate them.

Prepare the evidence kit before delivery day

Create a delivery folder while the parcel is still moving. Save the parcel number, tracking identifier, route name, submitted item list, packed weight and dimensions, declaration record, warehouse images and any parcel photo you purchased or received. These records describe what was supposed to leave the warehouse.

Add a simple expected-contents sheet. Give every product one row with its order or warehouse identifier, quantity, variation and any included component that could be mistaken for a separate item. A pair of shoes is one product; detachable straps, spare laces or accessories may still matter when checking completeness. Keep the list short enough to use while unpacking.

Charge the recording device, clear storage space and choose a well-lit surface large enough to keep the entire carton in frame. If the parcel goes to a family member, reception desk or forwarding address, share the inspection plan in advance. The first person handling the box controls the earliest evidence.

Inspect the parcel before acceptance

Begin without opening anything. Look at all six faces of the carton or mailer. Check for crushing, punctures, wet areas, retaping, broken seals, open seams or a label that appears detached and reapplied. Compare the recipient and tracking details with your expected shipment while shielding personal address data from any video you later share publicly.

If practical, photograph the parcel on a scale. Compare the reading cautiously with the recorded packed weight because carrier labels, local units and scale accuracy can create small differences. The useful signal is an obvious unexplained gap, especially when combined with damaged packaging. Do not rewrite or cover the original label.

Local delivery practices differ, so ask the courier or pickup point how damage can be noted before signing. If the package is severely compromised, preserve the condition and request the available exception record. Do not make a confrontation or assume refusal is always the correct legal step. The aim is a time-stamped carrier-side observation where the local process supports one.

Delivery gate: Before the seal is broken, capture the tracking label, every side of the parcel, the original closure, visible damage and a weight reading when one can be obtained safely.

Record one continuous unboxing

Start the video with the unopened box. Slowly show the label, corners, seams and seal, then open it while the parcel remains visible. A fixed camera is usually stronger than a handheld close-up because it shows that nothing entered or left the scene. Avoid pauses, cuts and moving the box off-screen during the critical sequence.

Remove protective material in layers. Show the top layer before lifting it, then place each product in a separate visible area. Read or display the warehouse identifier when it is present. Count multiples aloud and show empty cavities, torn inner bags or loose components before rearranging them. If a problem appears, keep recording long enough to establish its context.

Do not force a damaged item to function for the camera. For electronics, liquids, batteries or sharp breakage, stop if handling creates a safety risk. Record the visible condition and isolate the product. A claim file should never come at the cost of personal safety or further damage.

Reconcile three inventories, not just one box

A convincing shortage check compares three records: the items selected for parcel submission, the warehouse's packed or parcel record, and the goods physically removed on video. Mark each expected row as present, missing, wrong or damaged. This distinguishes a true parcel shortage from an item intentionally left in storage or placed in a different shipment.

Check nested packaging before declaring an item missing. Small accessories may sit inside a shoe, garment pocket or larger product box. At the same time, do not let “possibly packed elsewhere” become the final answer. Finish the controlled search on camera, show the empty carton and inner materials, then record the unresolved row clearly.

Use weight as supporting evidence, not proof by itself. Packaging variations and scale differences mean a number rarely identifies which product is absent. It becomes useful when paired with the manifest, continuous opening and an item-by-item count.

Classify the problem precisely

“My haul is wrong” is difficult to investigate. Place the event in one category. A parcel shortage means an item expected in this shipment is absent. A quantity shortage means some units arrived but fewer than recorded. Transit damage affects the box or goods during carriage. A product defect concerns the item's condition. A wrong-item case means an unexpected product or variation arrived. A lost-parcel case means delivery did not occur.

More than one category may apply, but do not blend them into a story. Write one sentence per issue: expected record, observed result and evidence filename. For example, identify the warehouse item, expected quantity, delivered quantity and the video timestamp where the empty parcel and count are visible.

This classification also routes the case correctly. A seller-side product problem, warehouse packing question and international carrier damage are not the same event. OOPBuy may need to review several stages, and its public terms indicate it can communicate with third-party logistics providers on parcel issues.

Build the support packet within 72 hours

OOPBuy's published help says to contact customer service within 72 hours after receipt for missing, damaged, defective and related product issues. Treat that as an action window, not extra time for casual investigation. Create the case as soon as the first controlled check is complete, even if support later asks for additional material.

Lead with identifiers: parcel number, tracking number, delivery date and route. Then state the exact issue category and affected warehouse or order IDs. Attach overview photographs, close-ups, the unboxing video, expected-contents sheet, relevant warehouse images and any carrier damage notation. Keep original files; compressed copies may hide detail or metadata.

End with a specific request: confirm receipt, review the packing record, identify any additional evidence needed and explain which line-specific after-sales standard applies. Do not demand a number copied from another buyer's case. OOPBuy's insurance and compensation page ties parcel loss or damage to each line's compensation standards, so route terms matter.

Preserve the physical evidence

Keep the outer carton, label, tape, seals, inner bags, cushioning and affected goods together. Do not discard, wash, repair or extensively test the item while the case is open unless support instructs otherwise or safety requires isolation. Photograph storage condition if the item could leak, deform or deteriorate.

Maintain one timeline: delivery attempt, acceptance, opening, problem discovery, first support contact and every later response. Name files consistently so a reviewer can connect a statement to evidence without searching through a camera roll. A tidy five-file packet can be stronger than fifty unexplained images.

If a carrier requests inspection or collection, confirm the process through the official channel before handing over goods. Record what was transferred, to whom and when. Preserve copies of every receipt or case reference.

Avoid evidence mistakes that weaken a real case

The first mistake is opening off camera and recreating the scene. A reconstruction may show damage but cannot establish arrival condition. The second is filming only the affected product; it omits the parcel, seal and item count. The third is editing the source video. Keep the original continuous file even if you create a shorter viewing copy.

Another mistake is waiting for certainty. You do not need to solve responsibility before contacting support. Report observable facts within the published window, label assumptions as assumptions and allow the records to be checked. Finally, avoid public posts that expose your address, tracking code or account information; evidence can be shared privately with support.

Use a five-gate delivery closeout

Gate one is package identity: the label and tracking number match. Gate two is exterior condition: all sides, seals and notable damage are recorded. Gate three is continuity: the unopened parcel and complete opening exist in one original video. Gate four is reconciliation: every expected item has a present, missing, wrong or damaged status. Gate five is escalation: any issue is submitted promptly with the route and evidence packet.

If all five gates pass and the goods are correct, archive the delivery folder with the order record. If a gate fails, repair what is still recoverable immediately. Photograph remaining packaging, write the timeline and contact support rather than delaying because the video is imperfect.

A careful delivery inspection is not pessimism. It is the final control in a long cross-border chain. The few minutes spent recording the sealed box, continuous opening and exact count can turn a vague complaint into a case that OOPBuy, the warehouse and the logistics provider can actually examine.

Frequently asked questions

How soon should I report a missing or damaged item?

OOPBuy's public guidance says to contact customer service within 72 hours after receipt for missing, damaged, defective or similar product issues. Submit the initial evidence promptly rather than waiting until the last hour.

What should the unboxing video show?

Show the unopened parcel, label, all sides, seals, opening sequence and every item removed. Keep the parcel in frame and avoid cuts around the moment the shortage or damage becomes visible.

Does visible damage guarantee compensation?

No. OOPBuy states that parcel loss or damage follows the compensation standards of the selected line. Evidence supports review but does not guarantee eligibility, outcome or amount.

Can I throw away the box after filming?

Keep the carton, label, seals and inner packaging until the case is resolved or support confirms they are no longer required.

What if someone else accepted the parcel?

Ask that person to preserve the unopened package and photograph its condition. If it was already opened, document the remaining evidence, build an honest timeline and contact support promptly without recreating events.
