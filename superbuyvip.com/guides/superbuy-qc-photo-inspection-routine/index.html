Superbuy QC Photos: What to Inspect Before You Ship

Turn standard warehouse photos into a repeatable inspection routine—and know when a paid detail photo is actually worth it.

QC playbookUpdated August 20261,674 words

Warehouse QC should answer a commercial question: is the item in front of the camera close enough to what you ordered that you are willing to pay to ship it internationally? That is different from admiring photographs, hunting for microscopic imperfections or expecting a warehouse to perform laboratory testing. A useful inspection is ordered, category-aware and tied to a decision while domestic remedies may still be practical.

Superbuy's current official homepage states that three free QC photos are taken after warehouse arrival. Its user guidance also describes quality inspection and storage before parcel submission. Those photographs create an evidence window, but they do not eliminate seller risk or reveal everything hidden inside a product. The routine below shows how to extract the highest-value information from limited views.

1. Define the inspection contract before arrival

Write down what “correct” means before the package reaches the warehouse. Record exact colour, size, model, count and expected accessories. Then identify two or three category-specific failure points. For a jacket, these might be chest width, zipper type and visible stains. For shoes, they might be size marking, pair symmetry and sole attachment. For a bag, they might be strap inclusion, closure hardware and corner condition.

This pre-arrival contract protects you from confirmation bias. Once a desirable item appears in photographs, buyers often lower standards or forget the original specification. A saved checklist gives the inspection a fixed reference and makes an exchange request easier to explain.

2. Understand what standard QC can and cannot establish

Standard images are strong at identity and visible condition. They can show colour family, count, exterior shape, obvious marks, labels that face the camera and whether a major accessory is present. They are weaker at precise measurements, subtle texture, internal construction, electrical function, scent, comfort and long-term durability. Lighting and camera angle can also alter perceived colour and proportion.

Never turn “not visible” into “confirmed.” If a clasp is hidden, the photograph does not prove it is undamaged. If the size label is too small to read, zooming until the image becomes pixelated is not evidence. Mark the question unresolved and decide whether it matters enough to request a targeted view.

3. Run the identity pass before the quality pass

Begin with the warehouse order record. Match the item name, selected colour, stated size, quantity and version. Check whether a set contains the expected number of pieces. Compare left and right items in a pair. Confirm that detachable parts mentioned in the listing appear in the photographs or package contents.

Identity errors outrank cosmetic flaws. A beautiful product in the wrong size or model is still the wrong product. Do not spend ten minutes reviewing stitching before confirming the visible size label. The identity pass should end with one of three labels: match, mismatch or not proven.

4. Read the scale and silhouette

Use the full-item view to judge proportions. Is the garment body unusually short relative to its sleeves? Are the two shoes similar in toe shape and height? Does a bag lean because of soft packing, or does one panel appear structurally distorted? Look for major asymmetry before studying details. Broad shape defects are usually more visible and more consequential than a single loose thread.

Do not infer exact dimensions from the warehouse table or background grid unless the photograph is intended for measurement. Perspective changes apparent size. If fit depends on a number, request that number directly rather than estimating it from a camera angle.

5. Follow a surface scan instead of random zooming

Scan each visible surface from top left to bottom right. Look for stains, scratches, colour transfer, dents, abrasions, glue marks, pulled threads and edge damage. Then repeat along high-stress zones: cuffs, hems, toe boxes, handles, corners, closures and attachment points. A systematic route reduces the chance that your eye repeatedly returns to the most attractive part of the product.

Separate packaging marks from product damage. A crushed retail box matters greatly to a collector but may be irrelevant to someone planning to remove the box for shipping. Define whether packaging condition is part of the purchase before you treat it as a rejection reason.

6. Inspect apparel for geometry, not just logos

For shirts, hoodies and jackets, compare sleeve lengths, shoulder alignment, hem level and print placement. Look at the way the garment lies flat: twisting can reveal panel or seam imbalance, although casual folding can also create distortion. Check visible size and care labels against the order. If fit is critical, request a specific measurement such as chest width, total length, sleeve length or waist across the garment.

For trousers, inspect waistband shape, leg symmetry, pocket placement and obvious fabric marks. Ask for one measurement at a time with clear endpoints. “Please measure the waist across the top edge while flat” is more actionable than “check if the pants fit.” The warehouse cannot know your body or preferred fit.

7. Inspect footwear as a matched mechanical pair

Compare both shoes side by side. Check toe profile, panel height, eyelet count, heel alignment and sole attachment. Look for major glue overflow, surface scratches, crushed structure and colour mismatch between the pair. Confirm the visible size marking. If the seller's sizing is uncertain, an insole-length photograph can answer a defined question better than another exterior angle.

Shoe boxes create a separate decision. If box condition or included accessories matter, confirm them before parcel packing. If freight efficiency matters more, plan whether to remove the box later. Do not ask the warehouse to discard packaging until you have completed the product inspection and decided what must remain protected.

8. Inspect bags and accessories at stress points

For bags, start with overall panel alignment, then inspect corners, handles, strap anchors, zipper tracks and closure hardware. Confirm detachable straps, pouches, dust bags or decorative parts only when the listing promised them. A bag stuffed for photography may look fuller than it will in use, so distinguish temporary shape support from construction.

For jewellery and small accessories, count pieces and inspect visible plating, stone placement, clasp type and scratches. Standard photographs may not prove metal composition or stone authenticity. For electronics, exterior QC does not equal functional testing. Treat any function, capacity or compatibility claim as unresolved unless a relevant service explicitly tests it.

9. Buy a detail photo only when it changes the decision

Superbuy's current fee guide uses CN¥3 detailed photos in its purchasing-cost example. Optional service prices can change, so confirm the live order interface. The important question is not whether a detail photo is cheap; it is whether the answer can prevent a more expensive mistake. A measurement that determines fit, a close view of a suspected stain or a photograph confirming an included component can have high decision value.

Write the request as camera instructions: identify the exact area, desired angle and purpose. “Photograph the left heel straight on, close enough to show the seam” is specific. “More QC please” is not. One well-designed request can be more useful than several generic images.

Decision-value rulePurchase extra evidence when the likely cost of being wrong—including product price, international freight and lost return flexibility—is meaningfully higher than the inspection cost, and when a photograph can actually resolve the question.

10. Classify findings by consequence

Use four levels. Critical means wrong item, wrong size, missing major component or damage that defeats the intended use. Material means a visible flaw you would not accept at the paid price. Minor means a cosmetic issue that does not alter use and may be tolerable. Unresolved means the available views cannot answer the question. This vocabulary produces clearer decisions than calling everything “bad QC.”

For every critical or material finding, capture the relevant photograph and explain the mismatch against the order record. For a minor issue, decide whether acceptance is still rational after considering price and return friction. For unresolved issues, either request evidence or consciously accept the uncertainty; do not let them remain invisible.

11. Decide before parcel planning

Accepted items can move into parcel scenarios. Exchange or return candidates should be addressed while seller-side after-sales options are still available. Avoid submitting a parcel simply because most items are ready. Once an item enters international delivery, a domestic correction becomes far more difficult and the shipping cost may exceed the value of the defect.

Record the date, outcome and reason. If you request another photo, set a follow-up. Superbuy provides 90 days of free warehouse storage under its published policy, but storage time is not a substitute for decision discipline. QC should be cleared soon after arrival so older items do not force rushed choices later.

12. The seven-minute QC sequence

  1. Match the order record and visible identity.
  2. Compare overall silhouette and pair symmetry.
  3. Scan surfaces in a fixed direction.
  4. Check category-specific stress points.
  5. Confirm promised accessories and visible labels.
  6. List unresolved questions without guessing.
  7. Choose accept, ask, exchange or return.

Repeat the same order every time. Consistency matters more than finding exotic flaws. It produces a defensible decision from limited evidence and makes your warehouse queue easier to manage.

Rebuilt questions buyers should ask

Do three free QC photos mean the item has passed a complete inspection?

No. They provide useful warehouse evidence, but photographs cannot establish every measurement, hidden component, function or long-term quality. Compare them with a prewritten inspection contract.

When is a paid photo unnecessary?

Skip it when the standard image already answers the question, when the issue would not change your decision, or when a photograph cannot verify the property you care about.

What is the most common QC mistake?

Inspecting attractive details before confirming identity. Always verify colour, size, version, quantity and major components first.

Can QC guarantee authenticity?

No. Visible warehouse photographs can support comparison with the listing, but they are not a universal authentication service. Treat authenticity claims as seller and product risk requiring appropriate evidence.
