Quality control guide

How to read Kakobuy QC photos before shipping.

A disciplined inspection is more useful than a fast “GL.” Use the warehouse window to verify the exact order, find visible problems and request only the extra evidence that can change your decision.

Reviewed September 22, 2026~12 minute readKakobuyLab Research Team

QC photos are the last practical checkpoint before a domestic purchase becomes an international parcel. Once you authorize shipping, a minor question can become an expensive return problem. The purpose of quality control is not to prove that an item is perfect or authentic. It is to decide whether the warehouse evidence matches the order well enough for you to accept the shipping risk.

What Kakobuy QC photos can—and cannot—prove

A warehouse QC set usually documents the received item from several standard angles. It can show the overall silhouette, selected color, visible branding, packaging, obvious stains, large defects and sometimes a measurement. That evidence is useful because it confirms that a physical item arrived and gives you a short window to react.

It does not provide a material test, authentication service or guarantee that every hidden defect has been found. Lighting can change color, lenses can distort proportions and compression can hide texture. Treat each photo as evidence with limits, not as a certificate.

Your first question should therefore be factual: did the seller send the color, size, model and quantity shown in the order? Your second question is visual: are there defects that matter to use, fit or appearance? Only after those questions are answered should you compare smaller details that are important to you.

Build a baseline before opening the gallery

Inspection is faster when you know what you bought. Keep the source listing, selected variant image, size chart and seller notes together. Record the exact color name, size, quantity and any accessory that the listing explicitly includes. If the seller offers many similar versions, save the thumbnail of the chosen SKU. Memory is unreliable when a page contains dozens of variations.

Separate promises from assumptions. If a product page does not promise a box, dust bag, spare laces or particular tag, their absence may not be a warehouse error. If a measurement chart allows a one-to-three-centimeter manufacturing tolerance, a small difference is not automatically grounds for return. The source listing defines the baseline; community photos can add context but should not replace it.

Practical rule: create a four-line note before inspection: item and version, selected color, selected size, and the one measurement or feature that would make you reject it. This prevents detail overload.

Use the same five-pass inspection every time

Pass 1: order identity

Confirm that the gallery belongs to the correct order and that the photographed quantity matches. Compare the product type, color family and selected SKU with your order record. Look for a size label if one is visible, but remember that a printed label is not the same as a physical measurement. If two similar orders reached the warehouse together, check package labels or distinguishing details so you do not review the wrong item.

Pass 2: overall structure

Look at the item as a whole before zooming into logos or stitches. For shoes, compare the left and right silhouette, toe shape, heel height and panel alignment. For clothing, check whether the body, sleeves, collar and hem sit evenly when laid flat. For a bag, look for a centered body, level handles and a shape that is not twisted. Severe structural asymmetry usually matters more than one imperfect stitch.

Pass 3: surfaces and construction

Now zoom in for stains, scratches, glue, loose threads, fabric pulls, broken hardware, chipped coating and print damage. Follow seams across the item rather than judging a single isolated point. A loose thread may be trimmed; a skipped seam or torn fabric is different. On knitwear, inspect snags and uneven panels. On coated surfaces, inspect corners and edges where transit damage often appears first.

Pass 4: measurements and fit risk

A tag tells you the seller’s nominal size, not the garment’s actual dimensions. If fit matters, compare a warehouse measurement with a similar item you already own. For tops, useful dimensions include chest width, body length and sleeve length. For trousers, waist, rise, thigh and inseam may matter. For shoes, an insole measurement is more actionable than the number printed on the size label.

Measurement photos must show the starting point, ending point and ruler position. A tape floating above curved fabric can add error. If the photo is ambiguous, request a repeat with the item flat and the tape aligned to a named seam. Avoid asking for every possible dimension; request the one or two that determine fit.

Pass 5: included parts and shipping readiness

Count detachable straps, laces, inserts, chargers or other components that the listing explicitly includes. Check whether fragile pieces appear protected. If you plan to remove a retail box to reduce volumetric weight, decide whether the item can travel safely without it. A small freight saving is not useful if it increases crushing or moisture risk.

Category-specific QC checks

Shoes

Compare both shoes from the front, side, rear and top. Look for mismatched toe height, heel lean, uneven sole bonding, panel differences, misplaced eyelets and obvious color variation between the pair. Ask whether the sole sits flat. For fit, prioritize insole length and, if relevant, width. A close-up of one decorative detail is less important than proof that the pair is symmetrical and wearable.

Lighting can exaggerate glue and make white materials look yellow or blue. Check the same area in more than one image before deciding. Some marks are reflections; others persist across angles. If a suspected defect appears in only one compressed image, request one clear close-up under normal warehouse light.

T-shirts, hoodies and jackets

Confirm front and back graphics, print placement, collar shape, zipper path, pocket alignment and sleeve length. A garment should be laid flat enough to reveal whether the body is skewed. For printed items, compare the print’s approximate scale and position with the chosen listing image. For embroidery, inspect missing fill, pulled threads and obvious distortion.

Fit is usually the largest risk. Compare measured chest width and length to a garment that fits you, using the same measurement method. Do not rely only on small, medium or large labels across sellers. Heavyweight hoodies and lined jackets can also affect international shipping more than the product price suggests, so note the warehouse weight before parcel planning.

Bags and accessories

Inspect the overall shape with the item standing and, when useful, laid flat. Check handle attachment, strap hardware, zipper movement, corner wear and major pattern or panel alignment. If the item includes a detachable strap, pouch or lock according to the order, confirm that it is present. For belts, verify total length and the distance to the usable holes rather than relying on the printed size.

Metal hardware photographs are especially sensitive to reflection. A bright streak may be light, while chipped plating tends to remain visible from multiple angles. Ask for a close-up only when the difference affects your decision.

Electronics and restricted items

Standard photos can confirm model, plug type, color and visible accessories, but they rarely prove long-term function. Check whether the device contains a battery or liquid because shipping-line eligibility may change. Confirm connector type, voltage markings where relevant and obvious physical damage. Functional testing, if offered, should be treated as a separate service with a clearly defined request.

Are QC photos free, and when should you buy an extra photo?

Standard warehouse QC images are part of the received-order workflow. The included count, availability and any charge for a special or additional photo are live service details. They can change with platform policy, item type or request method. For that reason, the order interface displayed after warehouse arrival is more reliable than an old screenshot or a fixed price repeated on a forum.

An extra photo is worth requesting when it can resolve a decision. Good requests target a hidden defect, a missing measurement, an uncertain variant or a part not visible in the standard set. Poor requests simply ask for “more photos,” repeat an existing angle or focus on a detail that would not change whether you ship.

Write the request so a warehouse worker can execute it without interpretation. Name the item area, desired angle, measurement points and whether packaging should be removed. For example: “Lay the hoodie flat, measure chest width from armpit seam to armpit seam, and keep the entire tape visible.” That is better than “measure the hoodie.”

Turn the gallery into a GL, exchange or return decision

“GL” means green light: you accept the visible condition and authorize the next step. It does not mean the item is flawless. Create three buckets. The first contains non-negotiable failures, such as wrong size, wrong color, major damage, missing promised components or measurements outside your acceptable range. The second contains tolerable variation, such as tiny loose threads, packaging dents or small differences within stated tolerance. The third contains uncertainty that one precise photo can resolve.

If a non-negotiable failure is clear, act within the seller’s return window rather than ordering more photos out of hope. If the evidence shows only tolerable variation, delaying may add storage time without improving the decision. If uncertainty remains, request the minimum evidence required and set a deadline for yourself.

Remember that domestic return freight, seller return policy and agent handling rules may affect the economics. A very low-cost item may not justify a complicated exchange, while an expensive or fit-sensitive item may justify additional checking. Quality control is a risk-management decision, not a hunt for theoretical perfection.

Common QC mistakes

The first mistake is inspecting branding before confirming the correct size and variant. The second is judging color from one image without considering lighting. The third is comparing warehouse photos with heavily edited marketing images at different angles. The fourth is treating every loose thread as equivalent to structural damage. The fifth is authorizing international shipping while an important measurement is still unknown.

Another mistake is outsourcing the entire decision to a short community comment. Experienced shoppers can point out patterns, but they do not know your fit tolerance, use case or budget. Use outside opinions as additional observations. Keep ownership of the final decision because you will pay the freight and live with the result.

The final 60-second QC checklist

  1. Correct order, quantity, color, size and version.
  2. Both sides or paired items look structurally consistent.
  3. No major stain, tear, crack, broken hardware or missing part.
  4. The critical fit measurement is visible and acceptable.
  5. Any requested extra photo answers a specific question.
  6. Packaging choices balance volume savings with protection.
  7. You have decided whether each visible issue is a rejection, tolerance or uncertainty.
  8. The return or exchange window is still open if action is required.

A good QC process is boring by design. It uses the same order, the same categories and the same decision rules every time. That consistency protects you better than zooming randomly through photographs or chasing someone else’s definition of perfect.
