LitBuy Custom Photo Guide: Turn Product Doubts Into Warehouse Evidence

A shot-brief method for converting one unresolved product question into a precise angle, measurement, comparison, or detail image before you accept or return the item.

Warehouse Evidence Guide12 min read1,773 wordsLast researched: September 7, 2026
Editorial scope: This independent guide was checked against LitBuy’s current Custom Photos help entry, Returns & Exchanges terms, and inspection disclaimer on September 7, 2026. A photograph documents visible evidence; it does not guarantee quality, authenticity, fit, seller acceptance, or a return.

Buy an answer, not another angle

The weakest custom-photo request is “take more pictures.” It sounds reasonable, yet it gives the warehouse no target and gives the buyer no test. Three extra images of the same attractive side may add pixels without reducing uncertainty. A useful request begins with a decision: accept, ask the seller, or pursue the available return path.

LitBuy currently describes Custom Photo as a value-added service costing one yuan per image. Buyers can specify an angle or location, and the resulting image is described as high-definition.

Write the doubt first. “Is the visible heel stitching continuous?” is a test. “Is the shoe good?” is not. “Does the chest width match the seller’s chart at the size I ordered?” is measurable. “Please check sizing” leaves the photographer to invent your standard.

Know what the regular set already covers

LitBuy’s Help Center says routine inspection generally provides three to six ordinary-pixel photos taken from the front, back, and side or logo area. Custom photography differs by giving the buyer more control over the requested angle, target location, details, and measurements. Use that distinction to avoid buying a duplicate of evidence already present.

Start with a contact sheet of the regular images. Under each image, write what it proves and what remains hidden. The front view may prove color blocking and general shape but not the back label. A side view may reveal profile but not outsole wear. A logo view may show placement but not the inside size tag. Only the unresolved column becomes a candidate for a custom shot.

Decision rule: do not request a new image unless you can finish this sentence: “If the photo shows X, I will do Y; if it shows Z, I will do W.”

Write a six-part shot brief

A warehouse photographer needs an instruction that can be executed without knowing your entire shopping history. Build it from six parts: item identity, target area, camera angle, reference or scale, visibility condition, and purpose. Keep the sentence short enough to translate cleanly.

For example: “For the black size-medium jacket, photograph the inside neck label straight-on, close enough that the full size and composition lines are readable; keep the entire label edge visible so I can compare it with the ordered specification.” The item is distinguished, the physical target is named, the camera is placed, the required detail is defined, and the intended comparison is clear.

Avoid relative words without anchors. “Closer,” “better,” “large,” and “correct” depend on the reader. Replace them with “fill most of the frame,” “show both endpoints,” “include the left and right item together,” or “place the ruler along the outer edge.” Do not combine four unrelated targets into one photo. Wide context and microscopic detail usually require different camera positions.

Measure with defined endpoints

A ruler in the frame does not automatically create a reliable measurement. The item must be positioned consistently, the ruler must follow the intended line, and both endpoints must be visible. Decide whether the product should lie flat, remain naturally shaped, or be gently stretched.

For clothing, name the seam or edge at both ends: armpit seam to armpit seam, shoulder seam to shoulder seam, or waistband top to hem. State whether the measurement is across the garment or around it. For footwear, distinguish outsole length, removable insole length, and internal space; they are not interchangeable. For bags, specify exterior width at the widest point, base depth, or strap drop rather than “bag size.”

Ask that the zero end and final reading remain visible in one frame and that the ruler sit in the same plane as the measured surface. A diagonal ruler floating above a curved object introduces perspective error. Treat the result as warehouse evidence, not laboratory calibration, and allow a sensible tolerance before turning a small photographic ambiguity into a defect claim.

Document a flaw with context and scale

A flaw image needs two jobs: locate the issue and show its severity. A very close photograph can reveal a scratch while making it impossible to tell whether the mark is on the toe, heel, or packaging. Begin with one contextual angle when location is unclear, then use a detail shot that includes a stable scale such as the nearby seam, edge, or ruler.

Describe observable features rather than conclusions. Request “the loose thread beside the left pocket seam” instead of “bad workmanship.” Ask for light across a dent, not “proof it is damaged.” Neutral language encourages a record that can be compared with the seller listing and later included in a return request if the item is eligible.

Do not ask warehouse staff to alter, clean, power, disassemble, or stress the item merely to produce evidence. A destructive test can change the product’s condition and complicate return eligibility. When the uncertainty cannot be photographed safely, label it unverified and decide whether that remaining risk is acceptable.

Compare pairs and sets in one frame

Some defects exist only as relationships. One shoe can look symmetrical until it is placed beside its mate. One sleeve can appear normal until both cuffs align. A set can look complete until every component is arranged together. For these questions, the custom shot should preserve the relationship instead of isolating the prettier item.

Ask for left and right products on the same flat surface, oriented the same way, at equal distance from the camera. Name the comparison line: heel height, toe shape, print position, handle attachment, color, or included quantity. If one piece is rotated or nearer the lens, perspective can imitate a mismatch.

For a multi-piece order, list the expected components in the request and ask for a single inventory frame before close-ups. The image should answer “Are all four listed pieces present?” before you spend another photo on a minor finish detail.

Separate color evidence from color certainty

Color is one of the hardest facts to settle through a screen. Warehouse light, camera processing, compression, your display, and the seller’s edited listing can all shift appearance. A custom high-definition image may offer more detail, but resolution does not neutralize lighting.

Ask for the entire product under even light, then identify any color reference that already belongs to the item: packaging, label, trim, or a second component. Avoid demanding an exact color match from a single photograph. Compare several stable regions, not highlights or deep shadows. If color accuracy is decisive, treat the image as a risk-reduction tool rather than absolute proof.

Texture has a similar trap. A close-up can show grain, weave, stitching, coating, or surface marks, but it cannot establish composition by sight alone. Record the label separately from the visible texture.

Respect the inspection boundary

Custom photography expands visual coverage; it does not expand the warehouse into a professional laboratory. LitBuy’s disclaimer says it cannot professionally inspect certain electrical appliances, branded goods, tickets, cards, and similar products for quality, authenticity, or completeness. Inspectors may verify external condition or accessories but cannot open devices to assess internal quality.

Use custom photos for visible serial labels, ports, accessory counts, seals, exterior damage, model markings, or switch positions. Do not describe those images as proof that a device works, a material is genuine, or an internal component is present. If the buying decision depends on an invisible fact, seller documentation or an appropriate specialist test would be needed; the photo request cannot manufacture certainty.

Sealed and collectible goods need an additional choice. Opening packaging may reduce value or affect return condition. Decide whether exterior evidence is enough before requesting a shot that requires removal. The safest instruction often preserves the seal and targets the visible label, corners, surface, and package identity.

Request evidence before the return clock runs

Photo review is a timed task when an item may need to go back to the seller. LitBuy’s current return page describes a five-day return-guarantee window for eligible products after they are stocked in the warehouse, calculated as 120 hours beginning from the next hour after the status becomes Warehoused. The guarantee is not universal: the seller must offer it, the product must remain marketable, and exclusions apply.

Record the Warehoused timestamp, calculate your internal review deadline earlier than the published boundary, and order only the decisive photos first. Do not wait for a perfect gallery while an after-sales option narrows. If the first custom image proves a material mismatch, move to the appropriate request rather than commissioning decorative angles.

Keep original packaging, labels, accessories, and documents intact while the decision is open. LitBuy requires approval before return and says unauthorized returns may not be accepted or refunded. A custom photo is evidence for a decision; it is not itself return approval.

Build a photo decision log

Give each requested image a short identifier. Record the order number, item, request text, unresolved question, image received, observation, and resulting action. Preserve the regular inspection set beside the custom evidence so the later record shows why another view was necessary.

Use four observation labels: confirmed, contradicted, unclear, and not photographable. “Unclear” should produce a revised request only when another safe angle can genuinely resolve it. “Not photographable” should feed the risk decision, not an endless photo loop. The buyer who knows what cannot be proven is in a stronger position than the buyer with twenty unspecific images.

Close the log when the product is accepted, held for seller clarification, or moved into an approved after-sales path. The purpose of Custom Photo is not to make every purchase feel certain. It is to spend one image on the question that still controls what you do next.

FAQ: LitBuy Custom Photos

How much does a LitBuy Custom Photo cost?
LitBuy’s current Help Center states that Custom Photo costs one yuan per image. Confirm the live service display before ordering because published services can change.

How is a Custom Photo different from regular inspection photos?
LitBuy describes routine inspection as generally three to six ordinary-pixel images from the front, back, and side or logo. Custom Photos are high-definition shots produced around the buyer’s specified angle, location, detail, or measurement.

What should I put in the request?
Name the exact item, physical target, camera angle, reference or scale, required visibility, and the decision the evidence must support. One photo should normally answer one question.

Can a custom photo prove authenticity or internal quality?
No. It can document visible facts. LitBuy’s disclaimer limits professional inspection of authenticity, internal quality, and completeness for certain products.

Should I order several general photos?
Review the regular set first. Request the smallest targeted shot that resolves the remaining acceptance question, then add another only if it supports a different decision.

Can I wait indefinitely before requesting photos or a return?
No. LitBuy describes a 120-hour warehouse window for eligible five-day return-guarantee items. Seller participation, product condition, exclusions, and prior approval still control eligibility.
